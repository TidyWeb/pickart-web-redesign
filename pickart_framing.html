Framing · A Position

We don't frame.
Deliberately.

The first reason is physics. A framed print travels under glass, and glass breaks. When it breaks in transit it does not break politely — it cuts the print it was fitted to protect. No courier can promise otherwise, and no amount of packaging changes this. An unframed print, rolled in an archival tube, arrives exactly as it left.

The second is craft. Framing is not the lithographer's trade. Our work ends at the edge of the sheet. Framing belongs to your local framer — a discipline with its own apprenticeships, its own tools, its own judgement. A good framer sees your print, your wall, and your light before cutting a single mitre. We can see none of those from here.

So we built this room instead.

The Frame

Five tones. Two profiles. One judgement.

A frame does not decorate a print. It ends it — a hard line between the work and the wall. The visualiser above holds the choice to what matters at this stage: five tones from black to white, a face from 10 to 90 millimetres, and a profile that is either flat or shaped.

The full field is wider than any preview can hold. Thousands of mouldings exist, and the right one depends on your print, your wall, and your light — three things only your framer can see. Take the tone and width you settled here; let the framer put a specific moulding in your hands.

One choice belongs to the glazing, not the wood. An archival print deserves UV-filtering glass or acrylic — it blocks the light that fades ink over decades. Non-reflective finishes trade a little crispness for a lot less glare, a judgement made in the room where the print will hang.

Ask for UV-filtering. Ask for spacers.
The Mount

Five ways to hold a sheet.

A mount does two jobs. It gives the image room to breathe, and it holds the print away from the glazing so the surface never touches glass. A window mat overlaps the sheet by a few millimetres on each side. A double mat steps two boards, a narrow reveal of the second colour ringing the window — depth measured in millimetres.

Float mounting reverses the logic. The whole sheet sits proud of the backing, every edge visible, a fine shadow beneath. A raised float lifts it further on a concealed riser, deepening that shadow to a drawn line. It is the right treatment when the margin carries the things that make the print what it is — the stamp, the number. Nothing is covered. Nothing is trimmed.

Every board is acid-free museum stock, hinged with acid-free tape, never dry-mounted. Whatever the style, one rule stands for a PickArt print: the embossing and edition number stay in view. That mark is the print's proof.

Ink can be copied. An embossing cannot.
The Framer's Brief

You've chosen. Now put it in writing.

When you finish in the framing room, one click produces the Framer's Brief — a single printed page carrying everything your framer needs: the sheet dimensions, your frame and mat choices, the glazing recommendation, and one instruction that is not optional.

Hinge, never dry-mount. The embossing and edition number remain visible. That mark is the print's proof. A framer who covers it has framed a picture and buried a document.

Take the Brief to any framer you trust. They do the part we never could.
